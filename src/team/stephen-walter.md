Stephen Walter leads technology and design at The Hope Group. He is the architect of Project Lookout, which he designed, developed, and project-managed with the Black Economic Council of Massachusetts, and of Driftwood. His work sits at the intersection of civic design, open-source software, and applied AI, and he leads the lab's work pioneering local, open-weight models inside the apps it makes.

He describes his practice through the idea of the *weird machine*, a computer science term for code that makes a system run outside the limits of its original design, producing unexpected outcomes. He applies the same idea to cities, communities, and organizations: find the small, provocative intervention that makes a complex system do something better than it was built to do.

He has been working as a designer, researcher, and program director at the intersection of technology, community, and care for the past 15 years. He has worked for organizations such as the International Red Cross and Red Crescent, the UN, the State Department, and the Boston Mayor's Office. He was also the founding managing director of the Engagement Lab, an applied research lab that used games and play to design more resilient social safety systems in communities around the globe.

Stephen's work has ranged from designing Upriver, an SMS-based cell phone game with residents along the Zambezi River, whose whole point was that by the time you finished playing it, you and the villages upstream had built a working early-warning network, texting river levels down the line; to Community PlanIt, an online platform for urban planning deployed in places from Detroit to Bhutan; to the Engagement Center, New England's lowest-barrier-to-entry harm reduction shelter for those struggling with homelessness and addiction. Currently he is the lead designer and developer of the [Community AI Project](https://communityai.studio), a constellation of free, open source AI tools for civic life built to take a deliberate step away from dependence on Big Tech. A former fellow at the Harvard Berkman Klein Center for Internet and Society, he has published peer-reviewed journal articles and book chapters on issues ranging from technology to public policy, including [*Meaningful Inefficiencies: Resisting the Logic of Technological Efficiency in the Design of Civic Systems*](https://drive.google.com/file/d/1Ven_npy5UZX5ZNiUDf6oYj2LbpXMO1B3/view?usp=sharing) from MIT Press.

At the lab he builds and runs the models, the pipelines, and the interfaces, and writes the plain-language explanations that ship with them.
